Home / Process

No surprises. No black boxes.

Every engagement follows the same discipline: find the real question before touching data, build evidence carefully, keep the client in the loop, and deliver work that holds up long after the engagement closes.

Why process matters

Most data projects go wrong before the first line of code.

The question is vague. The scope is tacit. The client is surprised at delivery. This process is designed to prevent that: every stage has an explicit deliverable, every deliverable is reviewed before the next stage begins, and the work is documented from day one.

The result is analysis that can be re-run, extended, and scrutinized — and a client who understands what the evidence can and cannot support when the engagement is over.

Six stages

From first conversation to final handoff.

Each stage has a concrete output. Nothing begins until the previous stage is complete and agreed.

01 — Contact

First conversation

Describe the decision you are facing and what data you have. Brian will ask clarifying questions, give an honest read on whether analysis is likely to change the outcome, and flag any conflicts. For litigation matters, a conflict check happens before anything else. No pitch, no obligation, no preparation required on your side. The first conversation is free.

02 — Discovery

Find the real question

A structured session to review your data, any prior analysis, the constraints the work must satisfy, and who will act on the results. The stated question is often not the right one — this stage surfaces what is actually unknown, what the data can bear, and where the analysis could mislead. Deliverable: a scoping memo that defines the question precisely, identifies data requirements, and flags any feasibility concerns before money is committed.

03 — Plan of work

Written scope before work begins

A written statement of work: the question, the method, the deliverables, the timeline, and the fee — with explicit statements of what the analysis can and cannot answer. Milestones and client-review checkpoints are built into the schedule. Any expansion of scope during execution requires a written change order. Nothing begins until the plan is agreed and signed.

04 — Execution

Iterative, with the client in the loop

Analysis proceeds in stages, with interim findings shared as they develop on a regular cadence — weekly or biweekly, on your calendar. If the data surfaces something unexpected — a different question, a data-quality problem, a result that reframes the engagement — you hear about it immediately, not at the deadline. Assumptions, limitations, and emerging findings are documented in real time. No black-box delivery at the end.

05 — Delivery

Reproducible outputs for the right audience

The primary deliverable — written report, expert opinion, model, or interactive tool — ships with everything needed to understand, re-run, and extend it: documented code, a data-lineage record, and a plain-language account of what the analysis shows and where its limits are. Visuals and narrative are designed for the specific audience: a board, a regulator, a jury, or a technical team.

06 — Handoff

You own everything

A final briefing session walks through the code, data, and documentation together. The deliverable is a versioned repository with a README, a data dictionary, and provenance records — everything a competent analyst on your team (or an opposing expert) would need to re-derive the result. The goal is not a report that sits on a shelf but work your organization can re-run, update, and extend independently.

Typical timelines

Scope determines duration. Most engagements run four to sixteen weeks from signed plan of work to final delivery.

Litigation and expert-testimony matters are paced by counsel's schedule. Strategy engagements can begin within days. Multi-phase research programs are milestoned and reviewed at each phase gate before continuing — you are never committed to the full scope before seeing what each phase produces.

What to bring

The first conversation needs almost nothing.

You do not need a fully-formed question, a clean dataset, or a fixed budget. Bring the decision you are trying to make and the uncertainty blocking it. That is enough to start.
